How to Write a Resume With No Experience (Examples)

Updated June 2026 · 6 min read

It feels impossible: the job wants experience, but you can't get experience without a job. The truth is that recruiters hiring for entry-level roles don't expect a long work history — they're looking for potential, attitude, and transferable skills. This guide shows you exactly how to build a one-page resume that gets interviews even with zero formal jobs.

The right structure when you have no experience

Flip the usual order. Instead of leading with an empty work-history section, lead with your strengths:

  1. Contact info
  2. Professional summary (2–3 sentences on who you are and what you bring)
  3. Education (degree, school, relevant coursework, GPA if strong)
  4. Projects (school, personal, or freelance — this is your secret weapon)
  5. Skills (tools, languages, soft skills)
  6. Volunteer work / extracurriculars / certifications

Write a summary that sells potential

Your summary replaces the experience a recruiter would normally scan first. Focus on your field, your strengths, and your goal. Example for a recent graduate:

"Motivated computer science graduate with hands-on experience building three full-stack projects and a strong foundation in Python and React. Eager to apply problem-solving skills as a junior developer on a collaborative team."

Turn non-job activities into achievements

You have more material than you think. Every one of these counts:

  • School projects: "Built a 2,000-user inventory app as a capstone project, leading a team of four."
  • Volunteering: "Organized a charity drive that raised $3,000 and coordinated 15 volunteers."
  • Clubs / sports: "Captained the debate team to a regional final, mentoring six new members."
  • Side work: "Managed a personal Etsy shop, handling listings, customer service, and shipping for 100+ orders."

Notice the pattern: each uses an action verb and a number. That's what makes a bullet feel like real experience.

Highlight transferable skills

Employers hire for skills, not just titles. Pull out the ones the job asks for: communication, teamwork, time management, specific software, languages. List them clearly, and back the important ones up with a bullet that proves it.

Keep it to one page

With limited history, one focused page beats two padded ones. Don't add filler — a tight, confident page signals that you understand what matters.

Frequently asked questions

How do I write a resume with no experience?

Lead with a strong summary, then education, projects, volunteering, and transferable skills. Use achievement-focused bullets with numbers.

What do I put if I've never worked?

Education, coursework, projects, volunteer work, extracurriculars, certifications, and skills — all of which demonstrate ability.

How long should it be?

One page.
